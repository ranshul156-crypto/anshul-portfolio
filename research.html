GutGlow

An artefact-aware multimodal wearable framework for continuous Crohn's disease activity monitoring.

  • Role First author · co-author Ajay Panchal
  • Stack Python · pandas · scikit-learn · matplotlib
  • Data Simulated longitudinal cohort (see below)
  • Status Feasibility study, 2026

I.

Motivation & research question

Crohn's disease is unpredictable in a way that makes ordinary life harder than it has to be: flares arrive with little warning. Wearable signals — heart rate, skin temperature, sleep — plausibly carry an early trace of that change. My question was narrow and testable: can a simple, artefact-aware model find that trace honestly — with no leakage, no in-sample optimism, and no demo numbers dressed up as science?

The clinical motivation is context for the engineering, not a substitute for validation. Nothing on this page is a clinical claim.

II.

Methods & system design

I built a simulated longitudinal dataset — 50 subjects × 90 days = 4,500 subject-days — with wearable-style signals and labelled flare activity, then an artefact-aware random-forest pipeline on top. The design premise is that wearables lie in specific ways: heart rate during motion, temperature during a shower, sleep when a strap loosens. I tried to encode that suspicion into the features and evaluation, not just into data cleaning.

Evaluation is subject-level: a subject appears in training or in test, never both. That single choice is the difference between this study's headline and its first draft.

III.

Limitations

Simulated data proves a pipeline can be built and audited; it cannot prove the signal exists in real patients at the strength the simulation assumes. The cohort is small, the covariates are mine, and the artefact model is a hypothesis about how wearables fail — the wearable prototype on the Engineering page is how I am testing that hypothesis at the bench.

So now I treat every synthetic result as a question addressed to real data, and I budget for the real-data study as the actual work.

Interlude

At ICMR

ICMR did not find me. I cold-mailed a bunch of government offices until one of them considered the project, and then I was presenting in New Delhi to people with PhDs after their names. For the first minutes I stuttered, and read their feedback as hostility. As it went on, I stuttered less — I started answering the question actually asked and conceding the point actually right. A line attributed to Nietzsche came back to me mid-feedback: the most dangerous form of blindness is when an individual thinks their worldview is the only worldview. It wasn't hostility. It was the work being taken seriously enough to be argued with.

So now I send the cold mail earlier, and I stay in the room longer before deciding criticism is rejection.

IV.

What's next

A small real-data study: real covariates, an ethics-approved design, and evaluation pre-committed to subject-level splits. The corrected subject-level result — 0.769 ± 0.055 — is the figure this work stands on.
