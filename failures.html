The day my quality metric didn't notice

A number I trusted to tell me when to believe the answer was not measuring the thing that had actually broken.

  • Subject Phone-camera photoplethysmography (PPG)
  • Measured Smartwatch optical (PPG) sensor · still vs. moving
  • Related Wearable prototype · GutGlow

No. 01 — the main exhibit

78 was true. 96 was confident. The metric saw no difference.

I built a small pipeline to read heart-rate variability from a smartwatch's optical sensor — the green light it shines into your wrist, watching blood volume change with each heartbeat. Nothing about the method was novel. The interesting part was what happened when I moved.

My still wrist gave a heart rate of 78 bpm at 18 dB of signal-to-noise — close enough to the 80 bpm I counted against my own wrist to be believable. Then I tapped my finger against the watch, and the algorithm returned 96 bpm: a number I knew was wrong, reported with total confidence. And here is the part I didn't expect, and still think about — the SNR barely moved. A metric I had trusted to tell me when to believe the answer was not measuring the thing that had actually broken.

Nothing in the code was wrong. The peak detector found the strongest periodic signal in the frame, which is exactly what I asked it to do. The strongest periodic signal was now my finger, not my heart.

A signal's quality and a signal's meaning are two different questions, and only one of them can be automated.

That single afternoon changed how I approach every measurement I make, because I had already made the same mistake once in a completely different place. I built a model that scored 0.984 and spent a happy week before I asked myself whether the number was too good. It was — the same subject had leaked across my train and test split, because I had used train_test_split without groups=. Rebuilt honestly — subject-level split, temporal smoothing — the current model reproduces AUC 0.980 with sensitivity of only 76.7% on held-out simulated subjects, and the repository is public so anyone can check that the split is clean. A high AUC with a sensitivity that misses a quarter of flare-days is its own lesson in reading past the headline number. That story is told in full on the GutGlow page; here it matters only because the shape of the error was identical. In both cases the fix was not an algorithm.

So now I check the thing that can't be automated: I count my own pulse against the screen, and I treat any result nobody could have predicted as a bug until it proves otherwise.

What I do next is also not software. Contact pressure, ambient light and the ADC's input bias are electrical problems, so the fix belongs in the front end — and in gating the measurement on motion rather than forgiving it afterwards. So now I reach for the front end first, and only then for the model.

Coda

The fix I deleted

A smaller one, because not every failure arrives wearing a lab coat. Late one evening I found and fixed a real sync bug, by hand, in the editor — and then deleted the fix before it was ever committed. The app kept running. The bug's transcript did not.

I shipped a working app and lost the proof that I had understood it. At the time that felt like a trade worth making. It wasn't defiance; it was tiredness making an engineering decision on my behalf.

So now nothing gets fixed at 10pm without being committed first — however ugly the patch is. A working app matters more than owning the transcript, but I no longer let those two things compete.

Why keep this page at all? Because a portfolio that only shows what worked leaves out the part of the work that taught me the most. First person, present consequence: every entry here ends in something I now do differently.
