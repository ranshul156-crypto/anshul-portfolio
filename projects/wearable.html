Project · Oct 2026 – present

Wearable sensing prototype

I was comfortable modelling other people's sensor data. I was not comfortable building a sensor. That gap is this project.

  • Role Self-directed, with friends who lent boards and patience
  • Hardware ESP / Arduino-class boards · thermistor front end · PPG
  • Software KiCad · serial logging · phone-camera PPG pipeline
  • Status In progress — smartwatch PPG test recorded

I.

Context

GutGlow runs on simulated data, and for a long time that was convenient in a way I didn't examine. I could tune a model all evening without ever touching the thing the model was supposedly about. My doctor-mentor wanted a physical prototype — something he could hold and react to — and I kept finding reasons the code needed one more week.

The honest name for that is procrastination. I loved electronics and was avoiding it, because code forgives you in private and hardware doesn't.

So now when I notice I'm polishing the comfortable half of a project, I treat that as the signal to go and do the other half first.

II.

Method

I messaged everyone I knew who owned a board. We put together a PPG sensor from borrowed ESP/Arduino hardware and parts from an old smartwatch, a thermistor front end on a voltage divider, and serial logging into a notebook. Nothing was bought new if it could be borrowed, which meant every session started with someone else's setup working differently from mine.

It short-circuited. It read noise. It read nothing. We ended up 3D-printing a mount and sealing the gaps with glue so sweat would stay out of the circuit — not elegant, but the first version that survived being worn.

When it finally returned a pulse, it felt less like a result and more like touching something I'd assumed would stay virtual forever. So now I prototype the physical version earlier and uglier — glue included — instead of waiting until the design deserves to exist.

III.

Failure

The smartwatch pipeline — green light into the wrist, brightness breathing with each beat — did something I didn't predict. Still, it read 78 bpm at 18 dB SNR, against 80 counted by hand. Then I tapped my finger against the watch and it returned 96 bpm with total confidence, and the SNR barely moved. The peak detector had simply found the strongest periodic signal in the light. That signal was now my finger, not my heart.

The full write-up, with the numbers as measured, lives on the failures page.

So now I count my own pulse against the screen before I believe any number the pipeline gives me. A signal's quality and a signal's meaning are two different questions, and only one of them can be automated.

IV.

What's next

Artefact rejection where it belongs — at the front end, gated on motion and contact pressure and ambient light — instead of forgiveness applied afterwards in software. The measurement is recorded; the gating is the work that remains.

Contact pressure, ambient light, and input bias are electrical problems. So now I budget for them as electrical problems, with a soldering iron, before I budget for them as code.
